# Routine 02 · Input · Procurement request handling
## As described by Tariq Nasser, Graduate, Group Procurement
*(Synthetic. All names, projects, figures and processes are fictional and created for training purposes only. Illustrative scenario data, not Modon operational data.)*

---

Requests come in three ways. Most are emails. Some arrive on Teams. A fair number are someone stopping me in the corridor and saying "can you get us three of the same thing as last time", which I then have to turn into an actual request myself.

The first thing I do is log it in my spreadsheet: date, requester, what, rough value, cost centre. About half the requests are missing something I need — usually the cost centre or the budget line, sometimes the spec is just "a laptop". So I go back to the requester. On average that takes two rounds. The record is five.

Then I check the vendor. We have an approved vendor list, which is a PDF someone sent me in March. If the vendor is on it, fine. If not, there is a form. Anything over AED 50,000 needs three quotes and requesters almost never know that, so that is another round trip, and usually a phone call because they are annoyed.

Then it goes for approval. Under 50,000 it is the line manager. Between 50,000 and 250,000 it is the head of function. Over 250,000 it goes to Daniel. Approvals are by email. I send it, and if nothing comes back in three days I chase. I keep a second spreadsheet just for chasing, because the first one got too wide.

Once it is approved I raise the purchase order in the procurement system. That is twenty fields. It is the same information that is already in my spreadsheet, typed again. If the vendor is new, I also have to set them up, which is another form and usually a week.

Then I wait for the supplier to confirm, update both spreadsheets, and close it.

I handle fifteen to twenty of these a week. Each one is maybe forty-five minutes of my actual time, but it takes eight or nine working days from request to purchase order, and the requester's experience is that procurement is slow.

Map this routine as a sequence of steps. Then find where the time actually goes.
